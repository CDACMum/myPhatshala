1 The story

The head chef who never cooks

It is the college fest, and the canteen must serve 3,000 plates of pav bhaji in two hours. Arjun Sir takes Riya and Kabir into the kitchen. Eight cooks are working at eight stoves. One person, the head chef, is not cooking at all. She holds a clipboard, calls out orders, and keeps looking at every stove.

R> Sir, why isn't the head chef cooking? With nine people cooking, we'd be faster. A> Watch what happens when cook 3 burns his hand. Who notices? Who gives his order to cook 4? Who knows that 2,400 plates are done and 600 are left? K> The head chef. Without her, the cooks would make the same order twice, or forget some. A> Exactly. A cluster works the same way. Many computers, the workers, do the real work. One or two computers, the masters, keep track of everything: which worker is alive, which piece of work is where, what is finished. R> And the workers tell the master they are still alive? A> Every few seconds. It's called a heartbeat. In Hadoop, a worker that stays silent for long enough is treated as dead, and its work is given to someone else. That's the whole lesson today: what makes a group of computers a cluster, and how masters and workers share the job.
2 Definition

What is a cluster?

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A cluster is a set of independent computers (nodes) connected by a network and managed by software so that they work together as a single system. In the master–worker (also called master–slave or coordinator–worker) architecture, one or more master nodes hold the cluster's metadata, monitor worker health through periodic heartbeats and assign work, while many worker nodes store data and execute tasks. Hadoop uses this architecture twice: in HDFS (NameNode and DataNodes) and in YARN (ResourceManager and NodeManagers).

In simple words

A cluster is a team of computers that behaves like one big computer. The master is the team leader: it remembers where everything is and gives out the work. The workers do the work and keep saying “I'm alive and here's my progress”. If a worker goes quiet, the master gives its work to another worker.

LEVELS> Clusters at three levels L1> A team with a leader
  • One leader (master) and many members (workers).
  • The leader keeps a list: who is present, who is doing what, what is done.
  • Members report in regularly (heartbeat). Someone who stops reporting is assumed absent, and their work is given to others.
  • To the outside world, the team looks like one unit: you give the job to the leader, not to each member.
L2> What the master keeps, and what the workers send
  • The master's table: worker list, last heartbeat time of each, resources free on each, which task is on which worker, and (for storage) which piece of data is on which worker.
  • The heartbeat: “I'm alive”, plus useful numbers: free disk, free memory, running tasks, finished tasks. In HDFS, DataNodes also send a full block report (the list of blocks on their disks) every few hours, and on start-up.
  • The reply: the master often answers a heartbeat with instructions: “start this task”, “copy this block to node 9”, “delete that block”.
  • Timeouts: the master doesn't declare a worker dead after one missed heartbeat; networks hiccup. It waits for a timeout, then acts.
L3> Design choices behind Hadoop's clusters
  • Master–worker vs peer-to-peer: Hadoop uses masters because one source of truth for metadata is simple and consistent. Systems like Cassandra are peer-to-peer (no master); every node is equal and they agree using gossip, which avoids a master bottleneck but makes coordination harder.
  • The master can be a bottleneck: the NameNode keeps all metadata in RAM (very roughly 150 bytes per file, directory or block object), so millions of tiny files can exhaust it. That's the “small files problem” (Module 7).
  • The master can be a SPOF: early Hadoop had one NameNode. Hadoop 2 added High Availability: an active and a standby NameNode with automatic failover via ZooKeeper (Module 7).
  • Shared-nothing lets the cluster grow just by adding nodes, unlike HPC clusters that share a central storage system.
  • Workers are cattle, masters are pets: a dead worker is replaced without fuss; masters are protected and backed up carefully.

3 Types and ways to use it

Kinds of clusters, and two ways to organise them

Big data cluster

Shared-nothing nodes, each with its own disks. Data is spread over the nodes and processed where it is stored. Hadoop, Spark, Kafka.

This course

HPC cluster

High-performance computing for science and weather. Very fast networks and a central shared file system; programs (often MPI) work closely together.

Supercomputers

High-availability (HA) cluster

Two or more machines where a standby takes over if the active one fails. Used for databases and for Hadoop's own NameNode.

Load-balanced web cluster

Many identical web servers behind a load balancer; each request goes to any free server. Used by shopping and ticketing sites.

4 Step by step

How a master and its workers run one job

  1. Workers join and register

    When a worker starts, it contacts the master: “I am dn4, on /rack2, with 32 cores, 256 GB RAM and 144 TB of disk.”

    The master adds a row to its worker table. In HDFS, the DataNode also sends a block report: the list of every block on its disks.

  2. Heartbeats every few seconds

    last_seen = {"dn1": 120.0, "dn2": 121.5, "dn3": 119.0}   # time of last heartbeat (seconds)
now = 122.0
for w, t in last_seen.items():
    print(w, "silent for", now - t, "s")
    dn1 silent for 2.0 s dn2 silent for 0.5 s dn3 silent for 3.0 s
    The master only stores when it last heard from each worker. Silence for a few seconds is normal. Silence past the timeout means trouble.

  3. A job arrives and is split into tasks

    A user submits a job on the master. The job is divided into many small, independent tasks, for example one per 128 MB piece of the input.

    Small tasks are the secret: if one fails, only that small piece is redone, not the whole job.

  4. The master assigns tasks to free workers

    Each worker gets as many tasks as it has free slots. The rest wait in a queue.

    In YARN, a “slot” is a container: a fixed amount of memory and CPU on one node. The master picks workers that are free and, ideally, already hold the task's data (Lesson 1.5).

  5. Workers report progress

    Heartbeats now carry “task 7: 60% done”, “task 3: finished”.

    This is how you see map 45% reduce 0% in your terminal: the master adds up progress from all workers.

  6. A worker dies: its tasks are re-run

    Worker 3 loses power. Its heartbeats stop. After the timeout, the master marks it dead and gives its unfinished tasks to other workers.

    The job finishes a little later, but it finishes, and nobody had to do anything by hand. That is the core promise of a cluster built from ordinary machines.
5 See it happen

A job with a dying worker: what Riya sees, and what the master does

Riya runs a job on a 3-worker cluster. Halfway through, worker 2 crashes. Left: her terminal. Right: the master's view.

Master, workers and a failure 6 steps
What you see
  1. Riya submits a job from the edge node. It is split into 6 tasks, one per piece of input.
  2. The job starts. No task has finished yet.
  3. Half the tasks are done: map 50%.
  4. Progress stops for a while. To Riya it looks stuck. Something has happened on the cluster.
  5. A message says a task attempt FAILED: “Container released on a lost node”. Then progress moves again.
  6. The job completes successfully. The failure was handled entirely by the cluster.
Behind the screen
  1. The master splits the job into 6 tasks and puts them in a queue. Each worker has room for one task at a time here.
  2. The master gives T1, T2, T3 to the three workers. T4–T6 wait.
  3. T1 and T3 finish. Worker 2 reported T2 done too, but its output lives on worker 2's disk. T4, T5, T6 start.
  4. Worker 2 loses power. Its heartbeats stop. The master waits for the timeout before deciding, because a short silence can be a network hiccup.
  5. Timeout: worker 2 is dead. Its task T5 was unfinished, and T2's output was on the dead disk, so both are re-run (T2′, T5′) on healthy workers.
  6. Every task is done. The job completes. The cluster healed itself.

6 Real-world use

Masters and workers everywhere

1. HDFS: NameNode and DataNodes

The NameNode knows every file's blocks and where they are. DataNodes store the blocks and send heartbeats every 3 seconds. You'll build and break this in Modules 5–7.

File→NameNode: where?→DataNodes store it

2. YARN: ResourceManager and NodeManagers

Every Hadoop and Spark-on-YARN job asks the ResourceManager for containers, and NodeManagers run them. One cluster can serve hundreds of jobs from different teams at once (Module 11).

Job→RM gives containers→NodeManagers run tasks

3. Spark: driver and executors

A Spark application has a driver (the master for that job) that plans the work and many executors (workers) that process the data. Same pattern, one level down.

Spark job→Driver plans→Executors compute

4. Kubernetes runs your favourite apps

Food delivery and ride apps often run on Kubernetes clusters: a control plane (masters) decides where each container runs; worker nodes run them and are replaced when they fail.

App containers→Control plane schedules→Workers run

5. Cassandra: a cluster with no master

Some databases, like Apache Cassandra, are peer-to-peer: every node is equal and any node can answer a request. It is a useful contrast when an interviewer asks why Hadoop chose masters.

Request→Any node coordinates→Replicas answer

Examples are simplified. Real deployments add more components (backups, monitoring, security) around these basic roles.
