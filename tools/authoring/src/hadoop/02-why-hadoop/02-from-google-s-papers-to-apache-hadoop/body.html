1 The story

Two research papers and a toy

Arjun Sir puts two old printouts on the table. One says The Google File System, 2003. The other says MapReduce: Simplified Data Processing on Large Clusters, 2004. “These twenty-odd pages changed the whole data industry,” he says. “Google didn't share its code. It shared its ideas. And two people building a free search engine read them very carefully.”

K> Sir, why would Google give away its secrets? A> Research papers are how engineers share ideas and get credit. The papers explained the design, but not Google's actual code. Anyone who wanted it had to build it themselves. R> And who did? A> Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella. They were building Nutch, an open-source web search engine, and had hit exactly the scaling wall Google described. They wrote their own versions: a distributed file system and a MapReduce engine. In 2006 that part was taken out of Nutch and given its own name: Hadoop, after a toy elephant that belonged to Cutting's young son. K> A toy! And now banks run on it. A> Yahoo! hired Cutting and put a large team on it, because they needed it for their own search. Within two years it was sorting a terabyte faster than anything else in the world. Today you'll follow that story year by year, and see which ideas from the papers became which parts of Hadoop.
2 Definition

Where Hadoop came from

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Apache Hadoop is an open-source implementation of the distributed storage and processing designs published by Google in “The Google File System” (Ghemawat, Gobioff and Leung, SOSP, October 2003) and “MapReduce: Simplified Data Processing on Large Clusters” (Dean and Ghemawat, OSDI, December 2004). It was created by Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella within the Apache Nutch web-search project, separated from Nutch as its own project in 2006, developed at scale largely at Yahoo!, and became a top-level Apache Software Foundation project in January 2008. HDFS corresponds to GFS and Hadoop MapReduce to Google's MapReduce.

In simple words

Google explained how it stored and processed the web in two famous papers, but kept its code. Two open-source developers rebuilt those ideas for their own search engine. That code became Hadoop, Yahoo! helped grow it, and the Apache Software Foundation made it free for everyone.

LEVELS> The history at three levels L1> The recipe and the restaurant
  • A famous restaurant (Google) published the recipe for its best dish, but not its kitchen.
  • A small team (Cutting and Cafarella) cooked the recipe in their own kitchen and shared their kitchen with everyone for free.
  • A big company (Yahoo!) paid many cooks to improve that free kitchen, because it needed to feed huge crowds.
  • Soon thousands of companies were using the free kitchen, and new dishes (Hive, Pig, HBase) were added on top.
L2> Paper idea → Hadoop part
  • GFS master → HDFS NameNode; GFS chunkservers → DataNodes; 64 MB chunks → HDFS blocks (64 MB in early Hadoop, 128 MB since Hadoop 2).
  • GFS kept 3 replicas by default, placed across racks → HDFS does the same.
  • MapReduce master → Hadoop 1's JobTracker; workers → TaskTrackers (replaced by YARN in Hadoop 2).
  • The paper's map, shuffle, reduce, combiner, partitioner, re-execution of failed tasks, backup tasks for stragglers all appear in Hadoop MapReduce.
  • Bigtable (2006 paper) → HBase.
L3> Why the open-source route won
  • Shared cost: Yahoo!, Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter and others all improved the same code instead of each building its own.
  • An ecosystem grew: Hive (from Facebook) added SQL; Pig (from Yahoo!) added a scripting language; HBase, ZooKeeper, Oozie, Sqoop, Flume and later Spark joined.
  • A business grew: companies such as Cloudera (2008), MapR (2009) and Hortonworks (2011, spun out of Yahoo!'s Hadoop team) sold supported distributions. Cloudera and Hortonworks merged in 2019.
  • Apache governance: no single company owns the project, so competitors could collaborate safely.
  • The pattern repeated: Google's later papers and systems inspired more open-source projects, and Hadoop's own limits inspired Spark (from UC Berkeley).

4 Step by step

How an idea in a paper became software on thousands of clusters

  1. 2002–03: Nutch hits the wall

    Nutch could crawl and index millions of pages on a few machines, but the whole web needed far more, and running it across machines was painful.

    Exactly the BharatSearch problem from Lesson 2.1: data too big, manual sharding, failures.

  2. 2003–04: read the papers, build NDFS

    The GFS paper described a design for exactly this. The Nutch team implemented it as NDFS.

    Key ideas copied: one master for metadata, many chunk servers, big chunks, replication across racks, the master never in the data path.

  3. 2005: add MapReduce

    After the MapReduce paper, Nutch rewrote its main algorithms (crawling, link analysis, indexing) as map and reduce jobs running on NDFS.

    Now storage and processing were general-purpose, not search-specific. That is why they could leave Nutch.

  4. 2006: Hadoop is born, Yahoo! commits

    The code moves into its own project called Hadoop. Doug Cutting joins Yahoo!, which assigns engineers to make it run on thousands of machines.

    Yahoo! needed it for search; the open-source licence meant every improvement was public.

  5. 2008: proven at scale

    Hadoop becomes a top-level Apache project, and a 910-node cluster wins the terabyte sort benchmark in 209 seconds.

    The record convinced many companies that free software on commodity hardware could beat expensive systems.

  6. 2011 onwards: releases and an ecosystem

    Hadoop 1.0 (2011), Hadoop 2 with YARN (2013), Hadoop 3 (2017), and a large ecosystem of tools and companies.

    Lesson 2.4 covers what changed in each version and why.
5 See it happen

From the GFS design to HDFS: what the paper drew, and what Hadoop built

Step through the main ideas of the GFS and MapReduce papers. Left: the idea as the paper describes it. Right: the matching part of Hadoop.

Paper idea → Hadoop part 6 steps
In the papers
  1. GFS: a single master keeps the file-system metadata: names and where each chunk lives.
  2. Files are cut into large chunks (64 MB) stored on many chunkservers.
  3. Each chunk is replicated (3 copies by default), with copies on different racks.
  4. MapReduce: programmers write a map and a reduce function; the system runs them on thousands of machines.
  5. The master detects failed workers and re-executes their tasks, and runs backup tasks for slow ones.
  6. Locality: the master tries to schedule map tasks where a replica of their input already is.
In Hadoop
  1. Hadoop's NameNode plays the GFS master's role: it holds the namespace and the location of every block.
  2. DataNodes store the blocks. Early Hadoop used 64 MB like GFS; Hadoop 2 raised the default to 128 MB.
  3. HDFS keeps 3 replicas by default and spreads them across racks, as in GFS.
  4. Hadoop MapReduce gives Java Mapper and Reducer classes to extend: the same two-function model.
  5. Failure handling from the paper is all there: heartbeats, task re-execution and speculative execution.
  6. And the locality idea, which you saw in action in Lesson 1.5.

6 Real-world use

How the early adopters used Hadoop

1. Yahoo!: search and the web graph

Yahoo! ran Hadoop on tens of thousands of servers for search indexing, the web link graph, ad analytics and spam detection, and contributed much of the core code.

Web crawl→Hadoop jobs→Search index, link graph

2. Facebook: SQL for analysts

Facebook stored huge volumes of user-activity data in Hadoop and created Hive so analysts could use SQL instead of writing Java MapReduce jobs.

Activity logs→Hive queries→Reports and insights

3. Social networks: “people you may know”

Professional and social networks computed recommendations such as “people you may know” with large batch jobs over their connection graphs, a classic early Hadoop workload.

Connection graph→Nightly batch jobs→Suggestions

4. Benchmarks that changed minds

In 2008 a 910-node Hadoop cluster sorted 1 TB in 209 seconds and won the terabyte sort benchmark. Free software on commodity servers was now competitive with expensive specialised systems.

1 TB of records→910 nodes→Sorted in 209 s

5. Distributions for everyone else

Banks, telecoms and retailers adopted Hadoop through supported distributions from companies like Cloudera, Hortonworks and MapR, which bundled HDFS, YARN, Hive, HBase and management tools.

Enterprise data→Supported distribution→Data lake
