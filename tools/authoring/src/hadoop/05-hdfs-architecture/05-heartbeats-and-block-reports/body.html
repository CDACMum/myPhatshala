1 The story

“Present, sir!” every three seconds

Kabir freezes datanode-4 with docker pause: the process is still there, but it can't do anything. He keeps asking the NameNode about it. “Live, last contact 10 seconds ago… 27 seconds… 43… 51…” Still live. Then at 57 seconds: dead. He unpauses it, and six seconds later the NameNode says “live, last contact 0 seconds”. Riya: “How does the NameNode know any of this? It never looks at the DataNodes.”

A> Like a class register. Every DataNode calls out “present” to the NameNode every 3 seconds: that's a heartbeat, with a few numbers about its disks and load. The NameNode just notes the time. If a DataNode goes quiet, the NameNode doesn't phone it; silence itself is the information. K> And how did it know what blocks datanode-4 has, after it came back? A> That's the second message: the block report. A DataNode lists every block it holds when it registers, then again every 6 hours, plus small updates whenever it gets or deletes a block. Heartbeats say “I'm alive”; block reports say “here's what I have”. And the NameNode's replies to heartbeats are how it gives orders: “copy this block”, “delete that one”.
2 Definition

Heartbeats and block reports

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A heartbeat is a periodic RPC from each DataNode to the NameNode (every dfs.heartbeat.interval = 3 s) carrying the DataNode's capacity, used and remaining space, number of active transfers and other load figures. It tells the NameNode the DataNode is alive, and the NameNode's reply carries commands for the DataNode, such as replicating a block to another node, deleting (invalidating) blocks, or re-registering. A DataNode not heard from for dfs.namenode.stale.datanode.interval (30 s) is stale; after 2 × dfs.namenode.heartbeat.recheck-interval + 10 × heartbeat interval (10 min 30 s by default) it is declared dead and its replicas are re-replicated. A block report is a list of all block replicas a DataNode stores (ID, length, generation stamp) sent at registration and then every dfs.blockreport.intervalMsec (6 hours); between full reports, incremental block reports announce replicas received or deleted. The NameNode builds and corrects its block map from these reports.

In simple words

Heartbeat: “I'm alive, and here's how full I am” every 3 seconds. Block report: “Here is every block I hold”, when starting and every 6 hours, with small updates in between. The NameNode answers heartbeats with jobs to do.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

LEVELS> Heartbeats at three levels L1> Attendance and inventory
  • Heartbeat = roll call: every 3 seconds each shelf-keeper says “present”, and how much space their shelf has left.
  • Block report = stock-taking: the shelf-keeper lists every book on the shelf. Done when they join, then every 6 hours.
  • The head librarian answers the roll call with tasks: “photocopy book 25 for shelf 3”, “throw away the extra copy of book 7”.
L2> What the NameNode does with them
  • Updates each DataNode's “last contact” and capacity, which dfsadmin -report shows.
  • Chooses targets for new blocks using reported free space and load.
  • Marks nodes stale, then dead, purely from missing heartbeats.
  • Rebuilds the block map after a restart from full block reports (Lesson 5.6).
  • Finds lost, extra or corrupt replicas by comparing reports with what it expects.
L3> Large-cluster details
  • The NameNode never initiates calls to DataNodes; all commands travel in heartbeat replies. This keeps one NameNode able to manage thousands of nodes.
  • Full block reports from big DataNodes (millions of blocks) are expensive; they are spread out over time and can be split per disk.
  • A busy NameNode may process heartbeats late; the optional lifeline RPC (dfs.namenode.lifeline.rpc-address) keeps busy-but-healthy nodes from being declared dead.
  • hdfs dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport host:9867 forces a full report, useful after disk repairs.
/LEVELS EXAM> “Explain heartbeat and block report”: heartbeat = DataNode → NameNode every 3 s, liveness + storage stats; reply carries commands; no heartbeat for 10 min 30 s → dead → re-replication. Block report = list of all blocks on a DataNode, at start-up and every 6 h (plus incremental reports); used to build the block map.

    The NameNode had forgotten this node, so on its next heartbeat it was told to re-register; then it sent a full block report, and its replicas were back in the block map within seconds (“lastContact 0”).

  5. Check and trigger a full block report

    docker compose exec namenode bash -c 'hdfs dfsadmin -report | grep -E "^Name|Last Block Report" | head -2'
docker compose exec namenode hdfs dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport lab5-datanode-2:9867
docker compose exec namenode bash -c 'hdfs dfsadmin -report | grep -E "^Name|Last Block Report" | head -2'
    Name: 172.19.0.4:9866 (lab5-datanode-2.lab5_default) Last Block Report: Wed Oct 21 06:49:03 UTC 2026 Triggering a full block report on lab5-datanode-2:9867. Name: 172.19.0.4:9866 (lab5-datanode-2.lab5_default) Last Block Report: Wed Oct 21 06:59:19 UTC 2026
    datanode-2's last full report was from its start at 06:49 (the next one was due 6 hours later). The trigger made it report at once. The command uses the DataNode's IPC port, 9867.
WARN> Don't lower heartbeat and dead-node intervals on real clusters just to “detect failures faster”. Short timers turn GC pauses and network blips into false deaths, which trigger mass re-replication and can overload the cluster. The lab uses short values only so you can watch.
5 See it happen

From live to dead and back

The real readings from step 3 and step 4, alongside what the NameNode is thinking.

datanode-4 goes silent 6 steps
What you see
  1. Normal state: datanode-4 checks in every 3 seconds and holds 8 blocks.
  2. The container is frozen. “Last contact” starts growing, but the node is still listed as live.
  3. After 30 s of silence it is counted as stale: still live, but suspicious.
  4. At about 60 s (lab settings) it is declared dead and drops out of the live count.
  5. Unfrozen, it heartbeats again, is told to re-register, and sends a full block report.
  6. Live again with all 8 blocks known. Block reports can also be triggered by hand.
Behind the screen
  1. While heartbeats arrive, the node is LIVE. Each heartbeat is also the NameNode's chance to send it work.
  2. The NameNode only measures time since the last heartbeat; it never pings the DataNode.
  3. After 30 s the node is STALE. Clients can be steered away from it if the avoid-stale options are enabled.
  4. After the dead interval it is DEAD: its replicas no longer count, and re-replication starts.
  5. When it speaks again, the NameNode asks it to register again, and its block report restores its replicas to the map.
  6. Back to LIVE. Block reports keep the map correct: full ones at start-up and every 6 hours, small incremental ones in between.

6 Real-world use

Heartbeats and reports at work

1. Alerts on dead and stale nodes

Monitoring reads NumDeadDataNodes and NumStaleDataNodes from the NameNode's /jmx. One dead node is routine; several at once usually means a rack or network problem worth waking someone up for.

/jmx→Dead/stale counts→Alert

2. Diagnosing flapping DataNodes

A DataNode that keeps going stale and coming back often has long Java garbage-collection pauses, a hung disk or network drops. Engineers compare its “last contact” history with GC and kernel logs.

Stale again and again→GC / disk / network→Fix the node

3. Block report storms

After a full cluster restart, thousands of DataNodes send full block reports at once. Big clusters tune report leases and splitting so the NameNode isn't overwhelmed, which shortens safe mode (Lesson 5.6).

Restart→Reports in waves→NameNode stays responsive

4. After disk repairs

When a failed disk is replaced or a data folder restored, admins run dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport so the NameNode learns the node's real contents now instead of in up to 6 hours.

Repair→Trigger report→Block map correct

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.
