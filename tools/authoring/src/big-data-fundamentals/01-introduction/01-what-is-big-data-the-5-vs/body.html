2 Definition

What is big data?

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Big data refers to data sets whose volume, velocity or variety is so large that traditional tools, such as a single relational database or a spreadsheet on one machine, cannot capture, store, process and analyse them within an acceptable time and cost. It is commonly described by the 5 Vs: Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity and Value.

In simple words

Big data is data that is too big, arrives too fast, or is too mixed for one computer and normal software to handle. It is often messy, and it's only worth collecting if you can get something useful out of it.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

RecordOne unit of data, such as one order, one payment or one click. A row in a table, or one JSON object.
Structured dataData with fixed columns and types, like a SQL table or a clean CSV.
Semi-structured dataData with labels but no fixed table shape: JSON, XML, log lines.
Unstructured dataData with no labels at all: photos, videos, audio, PDFs, free-text reviews.
ThroughputHow many records arrive or are processed per second. It measures velocity.
Data qualityHow correct, complete and consistent the data is. It measures veracity.

Two ways of counting: computers count in powers of 2 (1 KB = 1,024 bytes; the official names are KiB, MiB, GiB), while disk makers count in powers of 10 (1 kB = 1,000 bytes). That is why a “1 TB” disk shows about 931 GB on your computer.

Quick memory trick: each step up is about a thousand times bigger. “Kids Make Great Tea, Papa Enjoys Zero Yelling” gives KB, MB, GB, TB, PB, EB, ZB, YB in order.

LEVELS> The 5 Vs at three levels L1> Big data in your own phone

You already use big data systems every day. Each app below is dealing with all five Vs at once:

  • UPI: billions of payments a month (volume), each answered in about a second (velocity).
  • YouTube and Instagram: videos, photos, comments and likes together (variety).
  • Google Maps: live speed from millions of phones, some of them wrong or stale (veracity).
  • Shopping apps: “customers also bought” suggestions that increase sales (value).

Easy test: if one laptop or one database can store it and answer in time, it is not big data yet, however important it is.

L2> Putting a number on each V

Engineers don't say “we have a lot of data”. They measure each V:

VWhat engineers measureExample
VolumeTB stored, GB added per day, growth per year78 GB of new payments a day, growing 40% a year
VelocityEvents per second (average and peak), allowed delay (latency)4,000/s average, 30,000/s peak, reply within 1 s
VarietyNumber of sources and formats, how often their structure changes12 sources: tables, JSON, logs, images
Veracity% duplicates, % missing values, % failing checks0.4% duplicates from retries
ValueMoney saved or earned vs cost to store and processFraud blocked worth ₹2 crore vs ₹15 lakh platform cost

More Vs you may see: Variability (the rate or meaning changes, such as order spikes on festivals, or the word “cool” meaning cold or nice) and Visualisation (showing huge data so people can understand it). Some books list 7 or even 10 Vs; most university syllabi ask for 5.

L3> Peaks, value decay and the cost of keeping data
  • Design for the peak: the peak-to-average ratio (peak events per second ÷ average) decides capacity. A system that copes with the average fails on Diwali night or after a cricket final.
  • Value falls with age: a payment record is worth most in the first second (fraud check), less after a day (disputes), and much less after a year (audits, model training). So data moves from hot (fast, costly) to warm to cold (cheap archive) storage, and is deleted or anonymised when retention ends.
  • Law sets minimums: Indian anti-money-laundering rules require banks to keep transaction records for at least five years, and the RBI requires payment-system data to be stored in India. Retention and location are design inputs, not afterthoughts.
  • Working set vs total data: a company may store 2 PB but query only the last 30 days (40 TB) daily. Size the compute for the working set and the storage for the total.
  • Don't over-engineer: a 5 GB problem on a Spark cluster is slower and costlier than one good machine. The right question is always “which V actually breaks our current tool?”
/LEVELS EXAM> The first three Vs (Volume, Velocity, Variety) were described by analyst Doug Laney in 2001. Veracity and Value were added later by industry. If a question asks for “3 Vs”, write the first three; for “5 Vs”, write all five with one example each.
3 Types and ways to use it

The 5 Vs, one by one

1. Volume: how much

The total amount of data. Terabytes and petabytes, not megabytes. A bank stores every transaction for years because regulators require it.

Size

2. Velocity: how fast

The speed at which data arrives and must be handled. Thousands of payments per second at lunchtime, each needing an answer in a second or two.

Speed

3. Variety: how mixed

Different shapes of data: tables, JSON, logs, photos, voice notes, reviews. One system must store and use all of them.

Formats

4. Veracity: how trustworthy

Real data is messy: duplicates, missing fields, wrong values, fake accounts. You must clean and check before you trust a number.

Quality

5. Value: how useful

Data costs money to store. It is only worth it if it leads to decisions: stopping fraud, recommending products, planning stock.

Is it big data? A quick test

Not big data

A school's marks of 2,000 students. One MySQL table or one Excel sheet handles it easily, even with complex reports.

Small

Borderline

The canteen’s 13.5 lakh orders. Too big for Excel, but one laptop with Python or one database handles it in seconds.

Medium

Big data

All UPI payments in India, every second, with fraud checks in real time, stored for years. No single machine can do this.

Big

4 Step by step

Follow one ₹20 chai payment through the 5 Vs

  1. One payment becomes one record

    {"txn_id": "T1001", "payer": "riya@okbank", "payee": "canteen@upi",
 "amount": 20, "time": "2026-10-19 08:01:07", "status": "SUCCESS"}
    This record is about 130 characters. With extra fields that real systems keep (device, location, bank codes), a payment record is a few hundred bytes. Small. On its own, this is not big data.

  2. Volume: multiply by everyone

    per_record = 250                    # bytes
per_month = 10_000_000_000          # 10 billion payments
print(per_record * per_month / 1024**4, "TB per month")
    2.2737367544323206 TB per month
    About 2.3 TB a month just for the basic records, and banks keep them for years, with copies for safety. Add logs, fraud scores and app clicks, and you are into petabytes.

  3. Velocity: they don't arrive evenly

    per_second_avg = 10_000_000_000 / (30 * 24 * 3600)
print(round(per_second_avg), "payments per second on average")
    3858 payments per second on average
    Nearly 4,000 per second on average. At lunch, on salary day or during a sale, the peak is several times higher. Each one needs a fraud check and a reply in about a second.

  4. Variety: the payment is not alone

    Around the same payment, the systems also receive the app's click logs (JSON), an SMS (text), the canteen's menu photo, and Riya's 5-star review with a comment.

    One table cannot hold all of these. Big data platforms store raw files of any format in a data lake, and process each kind with suitable tools.

  5. Veracity: some records are wrong

    Riya's network dropped once, so the app retried and the same T1001 arrived twice. Another record has "amount": -20, and one has no payer at all.

    If you sum blindly, the canteen's income is wrong. Pipelines remove duplicates, reject invalid rows and log them for checking. Clean data first, numbers later.

  6. Value: turn records into decisions

    After cleaning, patterns appear: chai sells most at 8 am and 4 pm; samosas run out by 1 pm; one account is paying from five cities in ten minutes.

    The canteen stocks more samosas before 1 pm, and the bank blocks the suspicious account. Storing data has a cost; value is what pays for it.
5 See it happen

A ₹20 payment: what Riya sees, and what the data platform sees

Step through the six frames. Left: Riya's phone. Right: the same moment for the systems behind it.

One payment, five Vs 6 steps
What you see
  1. Riya scans the canteen's QR code and pays ₹20 for a cutting chai.
  2. A green tick: Payment successful. For her, the story ends here.
  3. Her bank sends an SMS and the app shows a notification, both within a second.
  4. She rates the chai five stars, writes a short review and adds a photo.
  5. Earlier that week, a payment showed Pending because the network was weak, and the app retried it.
  6. Next day the app offers cashback, and the canteen announces fresh samosas at the time they usually run out.
Behind the screen
  1. The payment becomes one record: a few hundred bytes of semi-structured JSON.
  2. Volume: about 10 billion such records a month is roughly 2.3 TB, kept for years with backup copies. That needs many disks on many machines.
  3. Velocity: payments arrive at about 4,000 per second on average, and far more at peak. Every one is checked for fraud before the reply goes back.
  4. Variety: the same moment produces a table row, JSON logs and text, and an unstructured photo and review.
  5. Veracity: the retry created a duplicate, and other records are broken. Adding them blindly gives a wrong total. Pipelines clean the data first.
  6. Value: clean data, grouped by hour, shows when samosas run out and who the regular customers are. Decisions follow.

6 Real-world use

Real-time scenarios: the 5 Vs under pressure

Three live scenarios: velocity breaking a single server on match night, all five Vs lighting up in the first seconds of a midnight sale, and an advanced one about how a record's value falls with age and where it should be stored at each stage.

Real-time scenario 1

Cricket final night at a food-delivery app

On a normal evening, orders arrive steadily. During a big cricket final, they come in waves: at the toss, in the innings break, and after the last ball. Watch velocity break a single database, and a big data setup absorb it.

Velocity on a match night 5 steps
Animated example
  1. Normal evening: orders arrive at a steady rate. One database server handles them comfortably.
  2. Toss: a small spike as people order snacks before the match. Still fine.
  3. Innings break: lakhs of people order at the same minute. The single server's queue starts to grow.
  4. Trouble: orders time out and customers see errors. A bigger server helps only up to a point, and is costly all year for one night's peak.
  5. Big data design: orders flow into a stream such as Kafka, and many machines process them in parallel. More machines can be added for big nights. Numbers here are illustrative.
Real-time scenario 2

Midnight sale: all five Vs in the first ten minutes

An online shop's big festive sale starts at 00:00. The data team sits in front of a live dashboard. Watch each V light up as crores of shoppers arrive at once.

Live 5 Vs dashboard 5 steps
Animated example
  1. 23:58: a normal night at about 3,000 orders per second. All five tiles on the dashboard are calm.
  2. 00:00:05: the sale opens. Orders jump to about 60,000 per second (velocity), and around a crore cart events pile up in ten minutes (volume).
  3. Variety: in the same second the platform receives payment JSON, search text, product photos and support chats. Each needs different storage and processing.
  4. Veracity: bots hammer the add-to-cart button and network retries send duplicate payments. They are filtered in the stream before any number is shown.
  5. Value: the clean, live numbers show Phone X selling out in Pune but not Mumbai. The team moves stock and pauses ads within minutes. (All numbers are illustrative.)
Real-time scenario 3 · advanced

The life of one payment record: value falls, so cost must fall too

Riya's ₹20 chai payment is stored for years. But it is not equally useful all that time. Follow the record as it ages and see where a sensible platform keeps it at each stage.

Value decay and storage tiers 5 steps
Animated example
  1. First second: the record sits in the stream processor's memory while the fraud check runs. Memory is the costliest storage, but this is when the record is most valuable.
  2. First days: Riya may check her history or raise a dispute, so the record stays in a fast hot database.
  3. After a month: it is used mainly for statements and analytics, so it moves to warm storage in the warehouse or lake.
  4. After a year: it is rarely read, but needed for audits and for training fraud models. It moves to a cold archive that costs a small fraction per GB.
  5. End of retention: once the legal minimum has passed (for example, at least five years for bank transaction records under India's anti-money-laundering rules), it is deleted or anonymised according to policy.

Where else the 5 Vs show up at work

1. Banks: catching fraud in real time

Every card and UPI payment is scored for fraud within moments: is this device new, is the location unusual, is the amount strange for this customer? That's velocity (answer fast), volume (years of history per customer) and veracity (don't block genuine payments).

Payment→Fraud model + customer history→Allow / block

2. E-commerce: “customers also bought”

Shopping sites record every search, click, cart and purchase from crores of users. Big data jobs find patterns overnight, and recommendations update as you browse. Variety matters: product photos, reviews and clicks are used together.

Clicks + orders→Spark jobs find patterns→Recommendations

3. Ride-hailing: surge pricing and ETAs

Cabs send GPS locations every few seconds. The platform combines millions of location points with ride requests to estimate arrival times and set prices zone by zone. That's velocity and volume at once.

GPS pings→Stream processing by zone→ETA + price

4. Telecom: network health from call records

Mobile operators collect a record for every call, SMS and data session, plus logs from every tower. Analysing them finds congested towers, dropped-call hotspots and customers likely to switch operators.

Tower logs + CDRs→Hadoop / Spark analysis→Fix towers, offers

5. Agriculture and weather

Satellite images (unstructured, huge) and weather-station readings are combined to estimate crop health, forecast rain and plan insurance payouts for farmers. Each image alone can be hundreds of megabytes.

Satellite + sensors→Image + time-series processing→Crop advice

Examples are simplified to show where each V matters. Real setups vary by company.
